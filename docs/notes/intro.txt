Why use a logistic / S-curve?

Imagine a bus leaves on 30 August, and booking starts on 10 August.

You probably wouldn't see:

10 Aug → 5 seats
11 Aug → 7 seats
12 Aug → 9 seats
13 Aug → 11 seats
...
30 Aug → 45 seats

That would be linear growth.

Realistically, something more like this could happen:

Date       Seats booked

10 Aug       2
12 Aug       3
14 Aug       4
16 Aug       6
18 Aug       10
20 Aug       16
22 Aug       23
24 Aug       29
26 Aug       34
28 Aug       37
30 Aug       38

Notice the pattern:

Slow → Fast → Slow

That's an S-shaped curve.

Why does this happen?
Early period

People don't usually book extremely early.

20 days before
      ↓
Very few bookings
Middle period

The trip gets closer.

10 days before
      ↓
More people start booking
      ↓
Bookings increase quickly
Near departure

The bus is getting full / remaining demand is limited.

1 day before
      ↓
Growth slows

So:

           _________
        __/
      _/
    _/
___/

That's why the logistic function is useful.


| Feature / Factor            | What it means                                 | Effect on bookings                                     | How we model it                   | Why it matters                        |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **Days before departure** ⭐ | How far the departure is                      | As departure gets closer → bookings generally increase | **Logistic / S-curve**            | Main booking-curve pattern            |
| **Midpoint**                | When the rapid booking phase happens          | Earlier midpoint → fills earlier                       | Logistic parameter                | Controls **WHEN** bookings rise       |
| **Steepness**               | How quickly bookings rise                     | Higher steepness → faster filling                      | Logistic parameter                | Controls **HOW FAST** bookings rise   |
| **Bus capacity**            | Number of seats available                     | Higher capacity → potentially more total bookings      | `bookings ≤ capacity`             | Prevents impossible predictions       |
| **Price** ⭐                 | Ticket price compared with normal route price | Higher price → lower demand                            | `Demand × price_ratio^elasticity` | Captures price sensitivity            |
| **Route popularity** ⭐      | How popular the route is                      | Popular route → more bookings                          | Demand multiplier                 | Mumbai–Pune ≠ small route             |
| **Holiday** ⭐               | Festival/public holiday                       | Holiday → higher demand                                | Holiday multiplier                | Travel demand increases               |
| **Day of week**             | Monday, Friday, weekend etc.                  | Weekend → generally higher demand                      | Day multiplier                    | Captures weekly patterns              |
| **Bus type**                | Sleeper, seater, AC, etc.                     | Can change demand                                      | Bus-type multiplier               | Different customers/preferences       |
| **Cancellations**           | Bookings removed before departure             | Mostly near departure → bookings can decrease          | Cancellation probability          | Creates realistic late dip            |
| **Random noise** ⭐          | Unpredictable real-world variation            | Sometimes ↑, sometimes ↓                               | Random multiplier/shock           | Prevents perfectly deterministic data |

> **Features → Effect on demand → Mathematical formula → Predicted bookings**

# Now the important part: What math are we actually applying?

Don't think of **one giant formula** initially.

Think of it as several steps.

## Step 1 — Base booking curve

We start with the **logistic function**.

A simple version:

[
B(t)=\frac{C}{1+e^{-k(t-m)}}
]

Where:

| Symbol | Meaning                        |
| ------ | ------------------------------ |
| `C`    | Maximum capacity               |
| `t`    | Time/progress toward departure |
| `k`    | Steepness                      |
| `m`    | Midpoint                       |

This gives us the basic:

```text
Slow → Fast → Slow
```

booking pattern.

---

# Step 2 — Apply route popularity

Suppose the base model says:

```text
Base bookings = 20
```

But this is a very popular route.

Assume:

```text
Route multiplier = 1.20
```

Then:

[
20 \times 1.20 = 24
]

So:

**Popular route → more bookings**

---

# Step 3 — Apply holiday effect

Suppose it's a holiday.

Assume:

```text
Holiday multiplier = 1.15
```

Then:

[
24 \times 1.15 = 27.6
]

Approximately:

```text
28 bookings
```

So:

**Holiday → more demand**

---

# Step 4 — Apply price effect

Suppose the normal price is:

```text
₹500
```

but your bus costs:

```text
₹600
```

Then:

[
price_ratio = \frac{600}{500}=1.2
]

Using elasticity:

[
Demand = Demand \times price_ratio^{-0.6}
]

So:

[
27.6 \times 1.2^{-0.6}
]

≈

```text
24.3 bookings
```

So:

**Higher price → lower demand**

---

# Step 5 — Add cancellations

Suppose you currently have:

```text
24 bookings
```

and near departure you expect 2 cancellations.

Then:

[
24 - 2 = 22
]

So:

**Cancellations → reduce final bookings**

---

# Step 6 — Add noise

Now suppose the mathematical model says:

```text
22 bookings
```

But real life isn't perfectly predictable.

Maybe:

```text
Expected = 22
Actual simulated = 20.8
```

or:

```text
Expected = 22
Actual simulated = 23.5
```

That's **noise**.

A simple way to represent it:

[
FinalBookings = ExpectedBookings \times (1+\epsilon)
]

where `ε` is a small random value.

For example:

```text
Expected = 22
Noise = +5%
```

Then:

[
22 \times 1.05 = 23.1
]

Or:

```text
Noise = -5%
```

Then:

[
22 \times 0.95 = 20.9
]

---

# So the complete idea is

You can remember this:

```text
                    DAYS TO DEPARTURE
                           ↓
                     Logistic Curve
                           ↓
                    Base Bookings
                           ↓
          ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
          ↓                ↓                ↓
    Route Popularity     Holiday           Weekend
          ↓                ↓                ↓
       Multiplier       Multiplier       Multiplier
          └────────────────┼────────────────┘
                           ↓
                       Price Effect
                           ↓
                     Cancellation
                           ↓
                         Noise
                           ↓
                  FINAL BOOKINGS
```

Or mathematically, conceptually:

[
\boxed{
FinalBookings =
LogisticBase
\times RouteFactor
\times HolidayFactor
\times DayFactor
\times PriceFactor
\times Noise

* Cancellations
  }
  ]

That's the **main formula to remember**.

---

## Why do we need noise?

This is VERY important.

Without noise:

```text
Features
   ↓
Exact formula
   ↓
Bookings
```

Your ML model can learn your formula almost perfectly.

Example:

```text
Actual = 25
Prediction = 24.99
```

You might get:

```text
R² = 0.99
```

But that's misleading because **you created the data using the formula in the first place**.

With noise:

```text
Features
   ↓
Formula
   ↓
Expected = 25
   ↓
Random real-world variation
   ↓
Actual = 23.7
```

Now the ML model has to learn the **underlying relationship** rather than simply memorizing an exact mathematical equation.

### So remember:

> **Formula gives the pattern. Noise gives uncertainty.**
